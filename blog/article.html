Website vs Social Media: Which Does Your Business Need?

By AlphaTNX Digital • Last updated 11 October 2026 • 9 minute read

Someone sees your post on Instagram or TikTok, loves the product, and sends you one message: "How much?" Then they wait for you. Maybe you reply in ten minutes. Maybe in three hours. But by then, they've moved on to a competitor whose price was already sitting on a page.

As a business owner, if that happens to you all the time, you probably don't have a visibility problem. Social media is doing its job: people are finding you. What's missing is a place where they can decide without needing you. So, when building a business in Nigeria, do you need a website, social media, or both? Here's the short answer, and how to choose what to build first.

So, which does your business need?

You need both, but they do different jobs. Social media gets your business discovered and keeps it visible. A website is where customers confirm what you sell, what it costs and how to buy, at any hour, without waiting for your reply. If you can only start with one, start where your customers already are, then build the website your social media points to.

The easiest way to remember it: social media is the signpost, and your website is the shop.

The numbers behind this question

It helps you to know where Nigerian customers actually spend their time online. According to DataReportal's Digital 2026: Nigeria report, 109 million Nigerians were using the internet at the end of 2025, which is 45.5% of the population. Social media is growing quickly too, with 47.8 million user identities in October 2025, up 34.7% in a year.

Messaging is where the real weight sits. Statista's Q3 2025 data shows WhatsApp reaching 96.5% of Nigerian internet users aged 16 and over, higher than any other platform in the country. In GWI's global survey, 98.3% of Nigerian respondents said they had used WhatsApp in the past month, the highest single-country figure for any platform in the study.

Two more findings are worth holding onto. DataReportal's July 2025 Global Statshot found that people are significantly more likely to visit a brand's website than to engage with that brand on social media. And about 130 million Nigerians were still offline at the end of 2025, so it's worth remembering that not everyone is reachable through a screen.

Put together, the picture is clear: Nigerians live on messaging apps, social media keeps growing, and people still visit business websites when they're serious about buying.

What's the difference between a website and social media?

A website is digital property you own and control. Social media is space you rent on someone else's platform. That's the difference that matters commercially.

On your own website, you decide what appears, how it's laid out and how long it stays. A price list you publish in 2026 is still there in 2028, on a page a customer can find in ten seconds.

On social media, you're a guest. The platform decides who sees your posts, how long they stay visible and how much of your own audience you can reach for free. Accounts can be restricted, removed or quietly deprioritised without much explanation.

Both can bring you customers. Only one of them is yours.

What a website can do that social media can't

A website can sell, explain, convince and rank in search without you being there. A feed moves on after a day. A website stays put and keeps working.

It can show your full catalogue in a structured way, with prices, ranges and packages that don't disappear after 24 hours. It can rank on Google for what you sell, so strangers find you while you're asleep. It can take orders and payments directly, with no back-and-forth chat in between, and it gives you one clear action per page, whether that's order, book, enquire or call.

It also builds credibility in ways social pages struggle to. A business email address like yourname@yourbusiness.com looks more serious than a Gmail address, and a website gives you one controlled place for case studies, results and reviews. It becomes the destination for every ad, QR code, business card and WhatsApp status you put out, and it stays up even if your social account is limited, hacked or banned.

A website doesn't replace your social pages. It finishes the job they start.

What social media can do that a website can't

Social media can put your business in front of strangers quickly, and for free, using content people actually want to watch. A brand-new website has no built-in audience. A social platform has an algorithm that can introduce you to thousands of people who've never heard of you.

It's also the easiest place to start. You can post today, from your phone, at no cost. Comments, DMs and shares build familiarity in a way a static page never will, and seeing other people engage with your business builds trust fast. It's where returning customers, referrals and word of mouth tend to live, and it's the quickest place to test an offer before you commit real money to it.

The honest summary: social media is unbeatable for getting attention, and weak infrastructure for holding it.

Do you need a website if you already have social media?

Yes, if your customers need information before they buy. The quickest way to find out is to ask one question: does a customer need to know anything before paying you? That could be the price, size, ingredients, delivery area, warranty, location, opening hours, past results, or simply proof that you're real.

If the answer is yes, and that information only exists inside DM conversations, you don't have a visibility problem. You have a conversion problem.

You don't need a website because "businesses should have one." You need one because customers who can't find answers on their own quietly leave, and you rarely find out they were ever there.

When social media alone is enough

Social media alone is enough when your sales don't depend on unanswered questions, and you aren't trying to grow beyond your current reach. That's a real situation, not a cop-out.

It usually describes a business that sells to people who already know the owner personally, with a simple product (one item, one price), where almost every sale happens on WhatsApp or in DMs anyway. It also fits the first few months of testing whether there's any demand at all, or volumes small enough that you can personally answer every message.

If that sounds like you, don't rush. Keep posting and keep selling.

Just know where the ceiling is. Once you're getting more enquiries than you can answer, competing with businesses customers can research faster, or paying for ads that lead nowhere, social media alone has stopped being a strategy and started being the bottleneck.

When your business needs a website

You need a website the moment customers start asking questions your social pages can't answer without you. The signs tend to show up in everyday work.

You might find yourself replying to the same questions every day: price, delivery, availability, location. Customers may ask "how much?" before they've seen anything useful. You could be boosting posts or running ads with nowhere solid to send people, or losing deals to competitors who are simply easier to research.

Other signals are about growth. You want to sell beyond the people who already trust you, you're outgrowing what one person can handle through messages, or you need credibility with corporate clients, suppliers or a bank. Maybe your account has been limited or hacked, and you realised everything you built lives on someone else's platform. Or you simply want to be found on Google for what you sell.

If two or more of these sound familiar, a website stops being optional. It becomes the cheapest fix available to you.

Which should you build first?

Build on social media first, then build the website it points to. Social media tells you what people want and where they are. A website that launches before you know that is a guess. Starting with social gets you customers and the language they use. The website then scales both.

In practice, the sequence looks like this:

  1. Start where your customers already are. For most Nigerian businesses, that's WhatsApp, Instagram or Facebook.
  2. Test what sells before you invest in presenting it properly.
  3. Write down the questions you keep answering. That list becomes your website's content plan.
  4. Build a simple site that answers those questions and offers one clear action.
  5. Point everything at it: your bio link, WhatsApp status, ads, business card, email signature and Google profile.

There's one exception. If you're already getting steady enquiries you can't keep up with, or you're paying for ads, build the website first. Both mean attention is arriving faster than you can convert it.

What this looks like for a Nigerian business

The winning setup in Nigeria is usually WhatsApp for conversation, social media for discovery, and a website for trust and selling. That's because local buying behaviour is message-first. Customers want to ask a question and get a fast, human answer. The goal isn't to fight that habit, it's to stop making it the only way to buy.

A practical stack has five layers. Social media (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok) handles discovery and personality. Your website carries prices, catalogue, delivery areas, proof and one clear way to buy. WhatsApp handles the conversation, confirmation and after-sale support. A Google Business Profile covers local search, hours, reviews and location. And a business email gives you credibility with serious customers and partners.

Each layer removes a reason for a customer to hesitate. Together, they turn attention into money instead of more unanswered messages.

One local reality deserves respect: not every customer has steady internet or unlimited data. That's an argument for fast, light pages, not an argument against having a website.

How to make your website and social media work together

Treat social media as the front door and your website as the room where the sale happens.

Picture the flow. Someone sees your post or ad and taps the link in your bio. They land on a page that shows what you sell, what it costs and how to buy. Then they either buy straight away, or they tap your WhatsApp button with a specific, informed question. You close the sale, and the next customer goes through the same steps without needing you to start from scratch.

That last part matters. A customer who arrives on WhatsApp already knowing your price, delivery area and options isn't a lead you have to teach. They're a buyer who just needs confirmation.

Here's a simple test: go from your Instagram page to a completed order, on your phone, as a stranger would. Count how many times you had to wait for a human. That number is your conversion cost.

What affects the cost of a business website?

The cost of a website depends on what it has to do, not on how it looks. Be wary of any quote that doesn't start with questions about your business.

The biggest drivers are how many pages you need (a five-page business site is very different from a fifty-page catalogue), whether it takes payments or only generates enquiries, and how many products you list (10 items versus 1,000 SKUs). Custom design costs more than a solid, proven layout, and integrations with a CRM, inventory, accounting or logistics tools add to the bill. Content matters too: whether your copy and photos are ready, or need to be created. Finally, there are ongoing needs like hosting, domain, email, updates and maintenance.

A useful rule of thumb is to price a website against the revenue it can recover, not like a design project. If a site fixes one recurring leak, such as losing customers who couldn't find your prices, it's usually worth more than it costs.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a website if I have Instagram?

Only if customers need information before buying. If your prices, delivery details or proof live only in DMs, a website removes a step that's costing you sales. If everything about your business is self-explanatory and your customers already know you, a website is optional for now.

Is a website better than social media?

Neither is better, because they do different jobs. Social media is better at getting attention. A website is better at converting that attention into sales, ranking on Google and staying under your control. Businesses that grow usually use both.

Can social media replace a website?

No, because you don't own social media. You can't control who sees your posts, your content expires quickly, and your account can be limited at any time. Social media can replace some marketing, but not ownership of your own sales channel.

Which is cheaper, a website or social media?

Social media is cheaper to start, since it's free. A website has upfront and ongoing costs for the domain, hosting and build. But social media gets more expensive as you pay for reach, while a website's cost stays relatively fixed as it keeps working.

How long does it take to see results from each?

Social media can produce attention within hours. A website takes longer to rank on Google, but it works continuously once it's built. If you need momentum this week, post. If you need a channel that keeps selling, build the site.

Do I still need a website if most of my customers buy on WhatsApp?

Yes, and this is the most common Nigerian case. A website doesn't compete with WhatsApp, it feeds it. Customers arrive already knowing what they want, so the conversation is shorter and closes faster.

What should be on my business website?

At minimum: what you sell, what it costs, where you deliver, how to order or pay, how to reach you, and proof that you're a real business. Add your Google Business Profile and a proper business email for extra credibility.

Can I start with just social media and add a website later?

Yes, and that's usually the right order. Validate demand on social media, note the questions you keep answering, then build a website that answers them and points to one clear action.

The bottom line

The real question isn't "website or social media." It's whether a customer can go from discovering you to buying from you without waiting for your reply.

Social media answers the first half. Your website answers the second. Most businesses need both, and most are only doing the first.
